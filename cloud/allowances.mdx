A cloud scan costs one scan. The rule is deliberately that simple: **every audit counts one, whoever or whatever started it.**

## Free and paid allocations

Your Projects screen shows the current allocation from the server. Signed-in Free personal accounts and Free Teams have recurring cloud allowances, separate from local project actions. [Free allowances](/free) explains active capacity and what remains readable after exhaustion.

The [pricing page](https://iriscode.co/pricing) reads paid plan quantities from the same backend configuration used by cloud accounting. Paid Teams shows the included-seat total and the amount per seat. The allocation in your Projects screen also identifies your current pool, usage and exact reset date.

Repositories start in manual mode. Free and paid users may explicitly enable merge or pull-request scans; automatic and manual scans spend the same applicable cloud pool.

## Paid and trial allocations

| | Window | Scans |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Pro** | per billing month | Current allocation shown in your account |
| **Teams** | per billing month | Current shared allocation shown in team billing |
| **Pro trial** | Trial period | As shown in your account |
| **Teams trial** | Trial period | As shown in the team |

## What counts as one scan

All of these, equally:

- An audit you start yourself from the project screen
- An audit triggered by a change landing on your default branch
- An audit triggered by a pull request being opened or updated
- The first audit queued when you import a repository

And these do not count, because they produced no report:

- An audit you cancelled
- One superseded by a newer push to the same branch or pull request
- One no scanner claimed, or that timed out
- One stopped because the repository was paused or GitHub access ended

## Reset dates by plan

Free allowances reset monthly, on the first at 00:00 UTC. Paid allocations follow their billing period; if that period starts on the 14th, its reset follows that period.

A trial has its own configured allocation and end date. Projects shows the exact date for the current pool. Starting paid access or a trial does not refill the underlying Free pool.

## Personal and team allowances are separate

Your personal account and each team have separate scan allowances. Unused scans cannot be transferred between them.

- Your account spends its applicable personal allowance
- Each team spends that team's allowance
- Being in two teams means two separate pools, both usable, neither shared

<Warning>
  A paid team seat does **not** add scans to your personal account. Team repositories use the team pool; personal repositories use your own Free, trial or paid allowance. You do not need a personal Pro subscription to use your personal Free scans. See [Team entitlement](/teams/entitlement).
</Warning>

## Running out

You are warned when 80% of the allowance has been used, the card on your Projects screen turns amber and says so, while there is still enough left to act on.

If it does run out, automatic audits stop for every repository in that account or team and each one reports "allowance used". Your GitHub connection is untouched, existing reports are unaffected, and audits resume on their own when the allowance resets. The screen names the date.

## Getting more

Three ways, depending on where you are:

- **Adding seats** adjusts the team's paid allocation according to its current billing configuration
- **Subscribing** replaces a trial allowance straight away
- **Auditing merges only**, rather than every pull request update, is the single setting that most reduces what you spend. See [Audit triggers](/cloud/triggers)

<Note>
  There is no scan top-up to buy yet. If an account or team runs out, the options are the three above or waiting for the reset.
</Note>

## Working out what you need

Estimate how many changes land and how often pull requests update, then compare that total with the current allocation in your workspace. Merges use one scan each. Keep manual mode if automatic activity would consume the allowance faster than you intend.

Turning pull request auditing on multiplies that by however many times a branch is pushed before it merges, which is usually three to five. That is the number worth estimating before switching it on across every repository.

<Tip>
  The separately configured daily audit limit per repository is a runaway-loop breaker, not a budget. It sits far above a busy day and exists so a misconfigured CI job cannot spend a month's allowance in an afternoon. Hitting it means something is looping, not that you are working too hard.
</Tip>
